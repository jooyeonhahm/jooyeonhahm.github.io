Hello, I’m Jooyeon (JOO-yun, /tɕuːjʌn/)! I’m the Head of Data Science Training and Consultation at [Stanford University Libraries](https://library.stanford.edu). I help students and researchers decide when computational methods, including machine learning and generative AI, can strengthen their work, when they cannot, and how to use them effectively at scale. My work includes developing researcher-focused training programs, advising on computational methods and reproducible workflows, managing shared research resources, building campus partnerships, and leading a team of seven research consultants.

Before becoming a data scientist, I trained as a historian. I earned my Ph.D. in History at the University of Pennsylvania and taught East Asian history at Penn, Yale, Wharton’s Lauder Institute, and Brandeis. When the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the academic job market, I began rethinking my career. I did not want to give up the questions I had been asking, so I looked for new ways to pursue them and earned an M.S. in Data Science at UC Berkeley. That transition continues to shape my work. I aim to foster methodological innovation in humanistic inquiries while bringing qualitative methods and sensibilities to data analysis.

At Berkeley, I studied generative AI in depth. I later worked as a software engineering intern at EBSCO Information Services, where I built a language-model pipeline to identify literature reviews in scholarly articles. These experiences in academic and industry settings prepared me for what I do now: helping researchers determine where large language models can genuinely contribute—for example, in classification, metadata creation, summarization, and deductive coding—and, just as importantly, where they cannot.

I advise researchers on the practical design of computational projects, from organizing data and selecting appropriate methods to making their workflows reproducible and, when needed, scalable. I also lead a team of seven research consultants from diverse backgrounds who provide tailored support to researchers across the humanities, social sciences, engineering, law, and medicine. Through workshops, tutorials, online guides, and individual consultations, we help researchers build data literacy and choose computational tools that suit their questions rather than allowing the tools to define the questions.

Teaching remains the part of this work I enjoy most. The instinct is the same whether I am leading a seminar on modern Japan or a Python workshop: make the room comfortable enough that people are willing to say what they do not yet understand. I rely on humor and plenty of hands-on examples. I believe the best learning happens at the edge of being challenged, where curiosity and uncertainty meet. My goal is to make that edge feel like a safe place to stand.

If you are working at the intersection of AI and research or simply want to talk through whether a particular computational tool is worth the effort, I would be glad to hear from you.
